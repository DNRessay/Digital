South African small businesses are at a crossroads. New funding pipelines promise cheaper credit, but a little‑known risk could undermine growth, and neighbouring Kenya’s payment shake‑up offers a warning about rapid tech adoption.

## A hidden threat could cripple South Africa’s SMEs
A recent Business Explainer piece flags a “hidden threat” that, if unchecked, may cripple the country’s SME sector – the backbone of the economy [1]. The article points to rising input‑cost volatility, especially in energy and logistics, combined with a tightening regulatory environment. For a typical shop owner or freelancer, sudden spikes in electricity or freight can erode profit margins faster than sales can grow.

**What it means:** If you rely on imported stock or run a physical storefront, you need to build a buffer. Short‑term actions include:
- Negotiating fixed‑rate electricity contracts where possible.
- Locking in bulk freight rates for the next 6‑12 months.
- Diversifying suppliers to avoid dependence on a single source.

Keeping an eye on policy updates from the Department of Trade and Industry will also help you anticipate regulatory shifts before they hit your bottom line.

## FNB’s referral partnership fuels SME funding growth
First National Bank (FNB) has struck a referral arrangement with fintech lender Sourcefin, aimed at channeling more small‑business loan applications through FNB’s existing customer base [4]. The model works like a two‑way street: FNB refers vetted SMEs to Sourcefin, and Sourcefin pays a referral fee while delivering faster credit decisions.

**What it means:** If you’ve struggled to get a bank loan, this partnership could open a quicker route to financing. Practical steps:
- Update your business profile on FNB’s digital platforms (e.g., FNB Business App).
- Prepare a concise one‑page pitch – revenue, cash flow, and purpose of the loan.
- Ask your accountant to certify recent financial statements; Sourcefin’s algorithm favours clean, up‑to‑date records.

## African Development Bank and Standard Bank seal a $332 million SME financing deal
The African Development Bank (AfDB) and Standard Bank Group announced a ZAR 5.4 billion (US$332 million) programme to expand credit for South African SMEs [6]. The funding will be channeled through a mix of direct loans, guarantee schemes, and capacity‑building workshops.

**What it means:** More money means more competition among lenders, which can drive down interest rates. Small businesses should:
- Register with the SME Development Fund (SMEDF) portal to access guarantee schemes.
- Attend the free webinars scheduled by Standard Bank on loan application best practices.
- Compare offers from at least three lenders before signing – the new pool should give you leverage.

## Good news for South African small businesses
A recent Businesstech article highlights several positive developments: lower transaction fees for card payments, expanded broadband subsidies for townships, and a pilot tax‑relief scheme for first‑time exporters [7]. While the piece is upbeat, the details are still being finalised.

**What it means:** If you sell online, the reduced card fees can improve margins instantly. For those eyeing export markets, start gathering the paperwork (HS codes, export licences) now so you can jump on the tax‑relief when it launches.

## Kenya’s digital payment boom leaves mobile money agents stranded
TechCabal reports that Kenya lost about 34,000 mobile‑money agents between March and June, a 5.6 % drop in registered agents [2]. The surge in digital payments has outpaced the support infrastructure, squeezing agents who traditionally earned commissions on cash‑in/out services.

**What it means for South African entrepreneurs:** If you’re considering a mobile‑payment solution, plan for agent support from day one. Offer training, transparent fee structures, and perhaps a small cash‑holding buffer to keep agents profitable.

## Copia Kenya ordered into liquidation – a cautionary tale
Copia, once hailed as Kenya’s logistics‑tech unicorn serving rural consumers, was ordered into liquidation after courts found rescue attempts exhausted [3]. The collapse underscores the difficulty of building profitable supply chains in low‑density areas.

**What it means for South African start‑ups:** If you target underserved townships, focus on:
- Lean inventory models (e.g., just‑in‑time delivery).
- Partnerships with existing logistics firms rather than building your own fleet.
- Clear unit economics – ensure each delivery covers its variable costs.

## ITC and Access Bank team up to unlock Africa’s SME growth
The International Trade Centre (ITC) and Access Bank announced a joint initiative to provide market‑access support, financing, and capacity‑building for African SMEs [5]. While the programme is continent‑wide, South African firms can tap into export‑readiness workshops and trade‑finance guarantees.

**What it means:** If you’re looking to sell beyond South Africa’s borders, register for the upcoming ITC‑Access Bank export bootcamp. Prepare a brief export plan (target market, product adaptation, logistics) to maximise the benefit.

## What to watch
The financing flood from AfDB, Standard Bank, and FNB’s referral model could tighten credit conditions for borrowers, so watch interest‑rate trends closely. At the same time, the hidden cost pressures highlighted in the Business Explainer remind us that cheap money won’t help if operating expenses explode. Finally, Kenya’s agent squeeze and Copia’s liquidation serve as a reminder: rapid digital expansion must be matched with sustainable on‑the‑ground support. Keep an eye on policy updates from the National Treasury and the upcoming South African Payments Association (SAPA) guidelines – they’ll shape how quickly new payment solutions can be rolled out safely.
